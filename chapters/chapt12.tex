\section{I Explore}
\section{Luminous and Non-Luminous Objects}
Objects that produce and emit their own light are called \textbf{luminous objects.} The light emitted by luminous objects can be seen from a distance and contributes to their visibility. Examples of luminous objects include stars, light bulbs, fire and lightning.
\vspace{0.1in}
\par
Objects that do not produce their own light but reflect light from an external source are called \textbf{non-luminous objects.} these objects become visible only when illuminated by a nearby light source. Examples of non-luminous objects include the Moon, planets, trees, buildings and most objects we see around us.
\vspace{1.0in}
\section{Transparent, Translucent and Opaque Objects}
\subsection{Transparent Objects}
\textbf{Transparent objects} allow light to pass completely through them. As a result, objects on the other side of a transparent material can be clearly seen. Examples of transparent objects include clear glass, clean water and clean air.
\subsection{Translucent Objects}
\textbf{Translucent objects} allow some light to pass through. So, translucent materials do not allow a clear view of objects on the other side. Examples of translucent objects include trace paper, frosted glass and muslin cloth.
\subsection{Opaque Objects}
\textbf{Opaque objects} do not allow any light to pass through them. Opaque materials block the view of the objects on the other side. Some examples of opaque are wood, metal and cardboard.
\vspace{1.4in}
\section{Shadows}
\textbf{Shadows} are formed when an object comes between a source of light and another surface. The object blocks some or all of the light rays and creates an area of darkness on the surface opposite the light source. The absence of light in these areas results in a shadow.
\par
The formation of shadows involves the following three key components.
\subsection{Source of Light}
The formation of shadows requires a source of light, such as the Sun, a lamp or any other light-emitting object.
\subsection{Object}
An object is required to block the light rays. This object can be opaque or translucent. Its interaction with the light determines the shape and chatacteristics of the shadow formed.
\subsection{Screen}
A screen is the surface on which a shadow is formed. It is just opposite the light source. It receives the light rays and when an object comes between the source of the light and the screen, a shadow is formed on the screen.
\vspace{0.8in}
\subsection{Properties of a Shadow}
The properties of a shadow depend on the size and shape of the object and the distance between the object and the screen. As the position of the light source and the screen, the size and the shape of the shadow also change.

\section{Eclipses}

\vspace{0.1in}

The shadows of Earth and the Moon called \textbf{eclipses.}
\vspace{0.3in}
\par

\vspace{0.8in}
\subsection{What are Eclipses?}
The sun is the main source of light. Earth and the Moon do not have their own light and sometimes block sunlight for each other. At times, the Sun, Earth and the Moon are in a straight line. This causes a shadow formation which blocks the view of the Sun or the Moon for some time. This is called an \textbf{eclipse.}
\vspace{0.1in}
\par
There are two types of eclipses-Solar eclipse and lunar eclipse.
\vspace{0.2in}
\subsection{Solar Eclipse}
During a \textbf{solar eclipse,} the Moon comes between the Sun and Earth. The moon blocks sunlight and casts its shadow on Earth. In the part of Earth where the shadow is cast, the Sun cannot be seen.
\vspace{1.5in}
\par
If only a portion of the Sun is blocked by the Moon, we call it a \textbf{partial solar eclipse.}
\vspace{0.1in}
\par
If the Sun is completely blocked by the Moon, we call it a \textbf{total solar eclipse.}
\subsection{Lunar Eclipse}
During a \textbf{lunar eclipse,} Earth comes between the Sun and the Moon. Earth blocks sunlight from reaching the Moon and casts its shadow of Earth is cast cannot be seen.
\vspace{0.1in}
\par
If only a portion of the Moon is shadowed by Earth, we call it a \textbf{partial lunar eclipse.} However, if the Moon is completely hidden behind the shadow of Earth, we call it a \textbf{total lunar eclipse.}

\section{Now I Know}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Luminous objects,} such as the Sun and a burning candle, emit their own light.
    \item \textbf{Non-luminous objects} do not emit their own light. They can only reflect the light from light-emitting objects.
    \item \textbf{Transparent objects} allow light to pass through them completely, \textbf{translucent objects} let some light pass through them and \textbf{opaque objects} block light and do not allow it to pass through.
    \item A \textbf{shadow} forms when an objects blocks light, creating a dark area on a surface. This object can be opaque or translucent.
    \item A \textbf{solar eclipse} happens when the Moon blocks the Sun, casting a shadow on Earth.
    \item A \textbf{lunar eclipse} occurs when Earth comes between the Sun and the Moon, creating a reddish glow on the Moon.
\end{itemize}
